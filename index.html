Experiments on The Life Engine

Is evolution
going in circles?

Run The Life Engine long enough and the same two shapes take over: armed plants — producers packed in a checkerboard, killers on the outside — and tiny movers built to dodge them and breed fast. We wanted to know whether that's forced by the rules, or just one history out of many. So we made the simulation fast enough to run hundreds of worlds, taught it what a species is, and drew its family tree as it grows.

What we built

Everything here runs in your browser. The source and every write-up are on GitHub.

10–20× faster

A simulation that isn't held back

The original was paced by the browser's timer and topped out around 170 steps a second on a typical board. It now runs in a background thread: 2,000–4,000 steps a second, and 13,000–28,000 across a dozen worlds at once.

173 → 7 species

Species that mean something

The original counted every mutant as a new species — 173 of them in one seeded world, which is really about seven. Now a species is everything within a quarter of its founder's body, and every lineage traces back to the very first organism.

eras & extinctions

History, detected from the numbers

An era is one species' reign. A mass extinction is a crash that wipes out at least half the established lineages. No guessing involved.

the map

What we found

The short answer: partly. Some of it is forced, some of it is luck, and the amount of food decides which.

01

The plant/animal split is forced. The arms race on top of it isn't.

Across 12 worlds with different seeds, mouths and producers always made up most of every body (mouths 25–39%, producers 28–51%). Everything to do with fighting varied far more: killers six-fold, armour from 0% to 16% — and at least one world never evolved eyes at all.

average body: mouth 29% · producer 41% · mover 11% · killer 11% · armour 2% · eye 6%
02

The armed-plant world came out of an extinction.

In one seeded world, an 83% population crash wiped out 29 of 33 established lineages — mostly grazers. Armed plants took over afterwards and made up 72% of all life. The shape from the top of this page didn't drift into being; it rose from the wreckage.

03

Food decides almost everything.

More food means smaller bodies: in every combination of settings we tried, the richest worlds had the smallest, and at high food every world converges on bodies of about three cells. Predators and eyes thrive only when food is scarce; with plenty they nearly vanish. And the default sits right at the edge: 3 of 20 worlds there went extinct at some point; none of 60 did with a little more food.

04

Scarcity is where worlds go their own way.

Plenty of food makes every world end up the same. Scarce food, with plants and animals allowed to mix, is where separate worlds take different paths and the most different body plans take turns ruling.

05

A grazer boom ended in collapse.

In a 120,000-tick world, grazers took over; then a mass extinction wiped out 46 of 56 lineages, almost all of them plants, and the grazers died out after them. Armed plants came back.

It looks like overgrazing — that's our reading of the sequence, not a measured cause.

06

Some shapes keep coming back.

In two separate worlds, the same three-cell grazer with an eye rose to rule, went extinct, and then showed up again as a new species.

Same cell counts, different species. Whether it truly evolved independently twice isn't verified yet.

07

Charging rent for every cell didn't help.

We added a metabolic cost per cell, scaled the way real animals' metabolism scales. At default food it made no measurable difference: it only slows births, and the gap between "too weak to matter" and "everything starves" is narrow. A null result — still worth knowing.

How sure are we? Worlds are noisy: the very same settings gave anywhere from 2.6 to 9.8 effective species across five seeds. Everything above is a result that clears that noise, unless it says otherwise. The full numbers are in the sweep write-ups.

What's next

  1. Rent at high food. Abundance is where every world converges on tiny bodies — a cost of existing might push back there, where it couldn't at default food.
  2. Terrain and climate. Right now every square of the world is the same forever, so each niche has exactly one answer. Gradients, seasons and barriers that rise and fall should give evolution more than one.
  3. Named eras. The detector already finds them; a local language model can tell their story.
  4. Brains that have to be earned. A neuron cell that costs upkeep, so intelligence only evolves where it pays — and you can watch the moment it does.
